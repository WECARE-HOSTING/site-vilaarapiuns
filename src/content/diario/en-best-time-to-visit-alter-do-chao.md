Alter do Chão has two entirely different years inside it, and which one you land in matters more than the month you choose. Most calendars for the Brazilian Amazon are written for Manaus, on the other side of the basin. This one is written from the Tapajós and the Arapiuns, where the beaches are.

## The two seasons, plainly

**The amazonian summer: August to January.** Dry, settled sun, little rain — and, crucially, **falling water**. As the rivers drop, sandbanks rise out of them. This is beach season, and it is what people picture when they see photographs of this region.

**The amazonian winter: February to June.** Rain, and **rising water**. The rivers climb several metres, the beaches vanish underneath, and the forest along the margins floods. What you get instead is the **igapó**: paddling a canoe inside the forest, between trunks and under canopy, over ground that was walkable in October.

Neither is the good season. They are different trips, and the mistake is arriving expecting the other one.

## Month by month

**August.** The water is falling and the first sandbanks appear. Warm, dry, and quieter than what follows. A good month if you want the beaches without the crowd.

**September.** Beaches established and growing. This is also **Sairé** — set for **17–21 September in 2026** — a festival that fills Alter do Chão entirely. Come for it deliberately or plan around it deliberately, but do not arrive unaware. Staying up the Arapiuns during Sairé is its own answer: close enough to go in, far enough to sleep.

**October.** Among the two best beach months. Low water, wide sand, reliable sun. Peak season.

**November.** The annual minimum for water level, which is the annual maximum for beach. Same weather as October, and in Brazil there are long weekends around 2 and 20 November that fill accommodation.

**December.** Beaches still full. School holidays start, northern-hemisphere travellers begin arriving to escape winter, and then the last week becomes **New Year** — Alter do Chão draws tens of thousands of people for the turn of the year, and lodging in the village goes early and expensive.

**January.** First half still good: beaches present, holidays running. Second half is the turn — the water starts climbing and the sand starts narrowing.

**February.** The beaches are going or gone. Carnival falls on 8–9 February in 2027. This is when we start describing the trip as forest rather than beach, and when people who want silence get it.

**March, April, May.** Full flood. No beaches at all. This is igapó season: the flooded forest at its most navigable, the light under the canopy at its strangest, and the river almost empty of visitors. It is also, for exactly that reason, when a group can have the whole place to itself — most of our retreat and private-group bookings sit in this window.

**June.** The water begins to fall. Beaches not back yet, but the season is turning and international traffic to the Amazon starts building.

**July.** Beaches returning, Brazilian school holidays, northern summer. Busy and good.

## Choosing by what you want

**Beaches and swimming:** September to December, with October and November best.

**Flooded forest and canoeing in the igapó:** March to May.

**Fewest other visitors:** February to May.

**A festival:** September, for Sairé.

**New Year:** the window is 28 December to 2 January, and it books out first.

**Wildlife:** the dry season concentrates animals near remaining water, which generally makes them easier to encounter — though we will not promise you a species list, because nobody honest promises wildlife.

## Two practical notes

**Book earlier than feels necessary.** International trips to Amazon lodges are typically planned three to nine months ahead. Retreat organisers lock a venue six to twelve months out. New Year is a single date.

**Stay longer than two nights.** Getting here is a full day from São Paulo and two from Europe. Three or four nights is where the arithmetic starts working, which is why our full-board rate begins at four.

## What does not change

The sun is strong all year — you are near the Equator, and the pale sand and clear water reflect everything back. And the beaches have no mosquitoes in any month, which surprises people enough that it has its own page.
