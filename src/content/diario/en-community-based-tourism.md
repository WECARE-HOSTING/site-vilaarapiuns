The phrase "community-based tourism" has become a label, and labels wear out fast. So it's worth separating what it actually means from what usually gets sold under that name.

## The difference that matters

The conventional model works like this: an outside company sets up an operation in a territory, hires a few local residents for operational roles, adds a "community visit" to the itinerary, and takes the revenue back to wherever it's headquartered.

Community-based tourism flips three things: **who runs it**, **who gets paid**, and **who decides**.

It's not a question of goodwill — it's a question of structure. You can be environmentally exemplary and socially extractive at the same time: solar power, zero waste, and the neighboring community reduced to scenery by the end of the day. The question community-based tourism asks isn't what the trip's footprint is. It's whose business this is.

## What the extractive reserve changes

The Arapiuns runs inside the **Tapajós-Arapiuns Extractive Reserve**, and that's not a bureaucratic detail — it's the legal foundation for everything else.

An extractive reserve is a **sustainable-use** conservation unit, and the decisive word is *use*. Unlike a national park, it was created to be inhabited: the traditional populations who already lived there have recognized rights to use the territory, under management rules they agreed on among themselves.

Two practical consequences.

The first: **the community isn't asking permission to work its own land**. The right is theirs.

The second, more concrete: the Arapiuns has a **formal Fishing Agreement** — a collective instrument that defines who fishes what, where, and when. That's why night spearfishing, done with a flashlight and a zagaia — a hand spear — is an activity the residents can teach: they have the right to fish there, under rules they helped write themselves. And it's the same reason it isn't, and can't be sold as, sport fishing.

## What this looks like day to day

No abstraction — what actually happens:

**The farinhada.** Cassava turned into flour, in the community oven. It's not a demonstration staged for visitors — it's what happens there every week, and you can join the process. Whoever teaches it is the one who does it.

**The weaving.** The artisans braid palm fiber and dye it with urucum. The piece comes off their hands, and the payment goes to them.

**The trails.** The walk to the samaúma tree starts from the community of **Atodi**, with a local guide, with residents. Whoever knows the path is whoever grew up on it.

**Spearfishing.** At night, by flashlight, freediving, with a zagaia. Learned from the people who fish.

**The kitchen.** River fish, flour from the neighboring communities, coffee brewed fresh. And the **piracaia**: fish roasted in the sand, in banana leaf, by candlelight, served in a circle by the team that lives here.

**Direct payment.** The activities run by the communities are paid to the families who run them — that's why we ask guests to bring cash. It's inconvenient to organize, and that's the whole point: the money doesn't pass through us before it reaches whoever did the work.

## Where we're not ready to promise yet

If this piece stopped here, it would be marketing copy. Two honest admissions.

**We haven't published the numbers yet.** How many families take part, what fraction of the team is local, how much of the activity revenue stays in the territory. The lodges that are the global reference for this model — Kapawi, in Ecuador, Posada Amazonas, in Peru — publish this as a line item, auditable. That's the right standard, and it's where we want to get to. Until we have a verified number, we won't make one up.

**We're not the only answer on the river.** There are **community guesthouses** in the Arapiuns communities, run entirely by the residents, much cheaper than the Villa. If what you want is to sleep inside the life of the river with the least intermediation possible, they're the most coherent option there is, and saying so is more useful than pretending they don't exist.

## What the Villa is, then

Thirteen bangalôs of tapajônica architecture on the bank of the Arapiuns, for up to 26 guests, with full meals and solar power. Operations and management are run by the riverside community; the activities, by the people who live there. From 15 people, the group gets the whole Villa.

It's community-based tourism with lodge-level comfort — and the reason we're writing this is that those two things rarely show up together, and when they do, it's almost always the second one that eats the first.
