This is the question that arrives most often, usually phrased apologetically, and almost always as the last obstacle before someone books. So: a straight answer, with the reasoning, because "trust us, there are no mosquitoes" is not an answer anyone should accept.

## The short version

On the beaches of Alter do Chão and the Rio Arapiuns, mosquitoes are close to absent. We serve dinner outdoors on the sand, without netting, without fogging, most nights of the year. That is not a marketing line — it is the daily operational fact that makes us willing to write this down.

On a forest trail at dusk, in the shade, you will meet insects. Use repellent there.

## Why the geography does the work

Mosquitoes are not distributed evenly across the Amazon, and the difference is not luck. Larvae need **still water** — and preferably still water rich in decomposing organic matter.

The Amazon that produces the reputation is made of exactly that: **blackwater igarapés** under closed canopy, where the water barely moves and the shade is total, and **várzea floodplain**, where muddy sediment-heavy water sits in shallow pools for months.

The Tapajós and the Arapiuns are a third kind of river. **Clearwater**, running over sand, with beaches that are open to sun and wind and washed by small waves. There is no still shaded shallow for larvae to develop in, and the water is nutrient-poor — the same low sediment load that makes it transparent also makes it thin food. The result is a shoreline that is genuinely quiet.

This is worth knowing for a reason bigger than comfort: it means "the Amazon" is not one experience. Many travellers decide against the region on mosquito grounds and never learn that the clearwater basins exist.

## Where the honesty belongs

We would rather be believed than impressive, so the qualifications:

**This is about the beaches, not the whole forest.** Walk the Samaúma trail late in the afternoon, into closed canopy, and you are in a different microhabitat. Repellent there is sensible.

**Season shifts things.** In the flood months, February to June, the water rises into the forest and creates the flooded igapó — beautiful, and more insect habitat than the dry-season sandbank has. The beaches themselves are underwater then, so the question changes shape.

**Other places on the same rivers differ.** A muddy inlet in a sheltered bay is not our beach, even a few kilometres away.

**We are not making a health claim.** Disease risk in the Amazon is a matter for a travel health service that knows your itinerary and your history. What we can tell you is what the evenings are like; what you need protection against is a question for a doctor.

## What to actually worry about

The sun. Every time.

You are near the Equator, on pale sand, beside water that throws light straight back at you. Guests who prepared for insects and not for ultraviolet spend the third day indoors. Hat, long sleeves, high-factor sunscreen, and shade in the middle of the day.

## Why we bothered writing this

Because the mosquito question is the single most common reason people talk themselves out of the Amazon, and on this particular river the fear is aimed at the wrong place. The trade-off here is real, but it is not the one you were expecting: not insects, but distance. The Arapiuns is 1h30 by boat beyond Alter do Chão, and that is the actual price of admission.

The beach is fine. Bring sunscreen.
